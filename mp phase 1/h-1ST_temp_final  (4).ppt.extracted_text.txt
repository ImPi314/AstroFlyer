An orbit is the curved trajectories of an object in space under the influence of gravitation. It is also known as an orbital revolution, because it is a rotation around an axis external to the moving body. Examples for orbits include the trajectory of a planet around a star, a natural satellite around a planet, or an artificial satellite around an object or position in space such as a planet, moon, asteroid or Lagrange point.. To a close approximation, planets and satellites follow elliptical orbits, with the center of mass being orbited at the focal point of the ellipse as described by Kepler
s laws of planetary motion.
Picture 5
Kepler
s laws of planetary motion Bodies following closed orbits repeat their paths with a certain time called the period. This motion is described by the empirical laws of Kepler, which can be mathematically derived from Newton's laws. These can be formulated as follows: Kepler's First Law (Law of Ellipses): It states that every planet moves in an
elliptical orbit
with the Sun located at one of the two foci. Kepler
s Second Law (Law of Equal Areas): It dictates that a line segment joining a planet and the Sun sweeps out
equal areas
during
equal intervals of time.
Kepler's Third Law (Law of Periods): It establishes a mathematical relationship where the square of a planet's orbital period is directly proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis of its orbit. This can be expressed by the formula: T2
a3 ,where T is the orbital period and a is the semi-major axis.

Difficulties faced in HEOs: A Highly Unstable Dynamic Environment Extreme Force Variance: Spacecraft in HEOs traverse entirely different physical environments within a single orbit. They face intense atmospheric drag at low altitudes and complex lunisolar gravitational perturbations at high altitudes. The Compounding Effect: These opposing forces couple together in non-linear ways. As drag shrinks the orbit over time, the timing of the gravitational pulls shifts dramatically. The issue with Event Prediction in HEOs: Due to this extreme sensitivity, standard trajectory models struggle to maintain accuracy over long durations. Minor fluctuations in solar weather or initial launch conditions can cause critical predictions
such as satellite lifespan, re-entry dates, and exact constellation coverage windows
to miss the mark by years or hundreds of kilometers.

The findings indicate that while the Tundra orbit provides significant advantages for polar coverage, its stability is highly sensitive to the resonance effects between the satellite
s orbital period and the Earth

The utilization of Highly Eccentric Orbits (HEOs) is vital for next-generation space missions, ranging from Lunar Gravity Assist (LGA) transfers to continuous polar Earth observation via Molniya and Tundra constellations. However, precise trajectory propagation and event prediction in these regimes present a severe astro-dynamic challenge. Spacecraft in HEOs traverse highly disparate dynamic environments within a single orbital period; they are subjected to dense, variable atmospheric drag at perigee, and complex third-body (lunisolar) gravitational perturbations at apogee. Current orbital models struggle to account for the non-linear coupling of these forces over long durations. Minor uncertainties in initial conditions or solar activity can compound rapidly, causing long-term event predictions
such as satellite lifetime and re-entry dates
to vary by decades. Therefore, to ensure the operational viability, precise spatial targeting, and regulatory compliance (e.g., space debris mitigation) of future HEO missions, a robust, high-fidelity trajectory propagation framework must be developed to accurately predict short-term observation events and long-term orbital evolution. .

Integrate Coupled Perturbation Models: Develop a dynamic modeling environment that accurately couples low-altitude atmospheric drag (incorporating variable solar activity models) with high-altitude third-body lunisolar gravitational perturbations and Earth's spherical harmonics (e.g., J2 effects). Analyze Long-Term Sensitivity and Orbital Lifetime: Quantify the sensitivity of HEO trajectories (such as GTOs) to minor variations in initial conditions and space weather. The goal is to accurately predict orbital decay, atmospheric re-entry windows, and long-term satellite lifespans for space debris mitigation compliance. Predict Precise Short-Term Trajectory Events: Implement numerical search algorithms (e.g., Newton-Raphson differential correctors) to precisely predict and target short-term orbital events, such as calculating exact B-plane parameters for Lunar Gravity Assist (LGA) transfers from highly inclined orbits. Validate Predictive Accuracy: Benchmark the developed propagation framework against established high-fidelity astrodynamics software (e.g., FreeFlyer, GMAT) or historical mission data to verify its computational efficiency and event-prediction accuracy over extended durations. Creating a high fidelity, robust Open-Source application that can simulate trajectory propagation at HEOs and the transfer maneuvers for any two given orbits whose parameters like perigee, apogee, and inclination are specified.
